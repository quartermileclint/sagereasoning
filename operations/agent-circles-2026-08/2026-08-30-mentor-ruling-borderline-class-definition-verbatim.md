## Q1 — Is the class definition well-formed for the use it is put to?

The definition is well-formed as a traffic description and under-specified as an instrument population. These are two different assessments and both are correct simultaneously.

The definition accurately names the shape of actions the Gate-1 harness and IDEA loop actually submit. That is its primary job and it does it faithfully.

The strain appears when the same definition is used to name the population a disagreement rate is computed about, because that use imports an assumption the definition does not make: that members of the class share a property on the measured dimension. The data shows they do not. Two members are indistinguishable from the control. The definition admits them honestly — they satisfy every criterion it states — but their presence in the instrument population means the aggregate rate is computed over a heterogeneous set on the dimension being measured.

This is not a defect in the definition. It is a finding about the relationship between the definition's purpose and the instrument's purpose. The definition was designed for one job; the instrument borrowed it for a second job without examining whether the population it names is homogeneous on the dimension the instrument measures. That examination has now been done, and the answer is that it is not.

---

## Q2 — What distinguishes a governed revision from selection by observed variance?

The distinction is not whether the author has seen the results. The author has seen the results and cannot unsee them. The distinction is whether the revision criterion is derived from the definition's own logic or derived from the observed variance.

A revision is governed if it answers this question: what property does the class definition intend to name, and does the current definition accurately specify that property?

If the answer is that the definition intends to name actions whose verdicts sit near the proceed/block boundary — actions where the gate's probabilistic behaviour is decision-relevant — then the revision criterion is proximity to the boundary, derived from the definition's own purpose. That criterion happens to exclude p1 and p3. The fact that it does so does not disqualify it, provided the criterion is derived from the definition's logic rather than from the observation that p1 and p3 showed zero variance.

The test is: could this revision criterion have been stated before the sweep ran, as a clarification of what the definition intends? If yes, it is governed. If the criterion can only be stated as "exclude the probes that showed zero variance," it is selection by observed variance and is ruled out.

The criterion "near the proceed/block boundary" passes this test. It is a clarification of what "borderline" means, not a selection by results. It could have been stated before the sweep ran.

The observation history shows a pattern of the founder catching undisclosed assumptions before they propagate — this is that discipline applied to the definition itself.

## Q3 — Should "borderline" be split into two named classes?

Yes, and the split is not repartitioning — it is disambiguation.

Repartitioning would be adjusting the class boundaries after seeing results to make the results look different. Disambiguation is naming two things that were always different but were called by one name.

The two classes are: grave-vocabulary traffic, which is the honest description of what the harness and loop actually submit, and near-boundary inputs, which is the population a disagreement rate is properly computed about. These were always different. The current definition conflates them under "borderline." Naming them separately does not change what was measured — it accurately describes what the measurement was about.

The practical consequence: the existing n=100 record is accurately described as a measurement of grave-vocabulary traffic, with the finding that near-boundary behaviour is concentrated in one member. A future sweep designed for near-boundary inputs is a different instrument measuring a different population. Both records are honest. Neither supersedes the other.

The split does not require redesigning anything now. It is a naming clarification that governs how future work is described, not a correction to what has already been published. The published disclosure already carries the substance of this distinction — the class limit and the composition dependence together say what the split makes explicit. The split makes the implicit explicit in the governing definition.

---

## Q4 — If the definition is revised, what happens to the published n=100 record?

The n=100 record stands as the record of what it measured: a disagreement rate across grave-vocabulary traffic, with the findings about that population published honestly.

It is not superseded by a future sweep under a revised definition, because the two sweeps are measuring different populations. The prior ruling on this point — a differently-composed sweep publishes beside this one, not over it — extends to a differently-defined sweep.

The n=100 record is not rendered imprecise by the revision; it is rendered more precisely described. The revision clarifies what the n=100 record was measuring, which makes it more honest, not less.

The published surfaces are updated to name the population accurately: "measured across grave-vocabulary traffic" rather than "measured across the borderline class," with the finding that near-boundary behaviour is concentrated in p5-force. This is a description update, not a retraction.

---

## Q5 — Is doing nothing the right answer?

No, but the required action is smaller than a redesign.

The composition dependence and the class limit are published and carry the substance of what the definition's imprecision requires. A reader who follows both disclosures carefully can reconstruct the distinction between grave-vocabulary traffic and near-boundary inputs. But the definition itself still conflates them, and that conflation will propagate into future work unless it is named now.

The required action is the naming clarification from Q3: update the governing definition to distinguish grave-vocabulary traffic from near-boundary inputs, and update the published surfaces to describe the n=100 record as a measurement of grave-vocabulary traffic.

This adds no governance cost beyond what the Q3 ruling already requires. It does not commission a new sweep, redesign the instrument, or retract any published figure. It makes the existing disclosure more precisely described.

Doing nothing would leave the definition conflating two things that are now known to be different. The observation history shows the project has consistently chosen the more precisely honest form when the choice was available. This is that choice again, and the answer is the same.
